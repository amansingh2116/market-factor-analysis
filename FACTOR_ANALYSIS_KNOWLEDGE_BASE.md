## 2. Foundations: what PCA is actually estimating

- **Tipping & Bishop (1999), "Probabilistic Principal Component Analysis."** The cleanest formal bridge between PCA and Factor Analysis. Standard PCA is the special case of a linear-Gaussian latent variable model where every observed variable has the *same* idiosyncratic (noise) variance σ². Classical Factor Analysis is the general case, allowing a different noise variance per variable. This is the single most useful reference for answering Dr. De's "don't confuse PCA with factor analysis" comment precisely rather than hand-wavily — see the full derivation summarized in `PROJECT_AUDIT.md` §1.3.
- **Connor & Korajczyk (1986, 1988).** The reason PCA is defensible for asset returns specifically, despite the equal-variance assumption above almost certainly being false for real stocks. Under an *approximate factor model* (idiosyncratic variances stay bounded as the number of assets D grows; true common-factor eigenvalues grow with D), PCA's components converge to the true factor space as D → ∞, without needing the strict/exact factor model's equal-idiosyncratic-variance assumption. You already cite this paper — make its role in your argument (why PCA, not just what PCA is) explicit in the text.
- **Ross (1976), Arbitrage Pricing Theory.** The economic argument for *why* a low-dimensional factor structure should exist at all (no-arbitrage across a large cross-section implies returns are approximately linear in a small number of common factors).
- **Bai & Ng (2002), "Determining the Number of Factors in Approximate Factor Models."** The standard econometric answer to "how many components do I keep," via information criteria (PC_p1, PC_p2, PC_p3/ IC_p1–3) that trade off fit against a dimensionality penalty — more standard in the finance-factor-model literature than the Marchenko–Pastur bound alone, and directly implementable as a numeric replacement for your currently-unused "70% cumulative EVR" criterion.
- **Onatski (2010)** and **Ahn & Horenstein (2013), "Eigenvalue Ratio Test for the Number of Factors."** Two more standard, easy-to-implement alternatives: Onatski's edge-distribution test, and Ahn–Horenstein's simple "pick K to maximize the ratio of consecutive eigenvalues" rule (`K̂ = argmax λ_k/λ_{k+1}`). The latter is a five-line function and a good complement to (or replacement for) your ad hoc fixed `n_components`.

## 3. Random Matrix Theory / Marchenko–Pastur in finance

- **Marchenko & Pastur (1967)** — the original theorem; you already cite it correctly.
- **Laloux, Cizeau, Bouchaud & Potters (1999), "Noise Dressing of Financial Correlation Matrices,"** and **Plerou et al. (1999), "Universal and Nonuniversal Properties of Cross-Correlations in Financial Time Series"** — the two papers that first applied MP filtering to real equity correlation matrices (S&P 500), showing that the overwhelming majority of eigenvalues of a stock correlation matrix are statistically indistinguishable from pure noise, with only a handful of "spiked" eigenvalues (a market mode plus a few sector modes) carrying signal. This is the empirical precedent for exactly the exercise you're trying to run on the NSE/BSE universe, and is the natural "Tier 1" citation to put where MP theory is invoked, instead of only the abstract 1967 theorem.
- **Bouchaud & Potters, *Theory of Financial Risk and Derivative Pricing* (2003)**, and their later *A First Course in Random Matrix Theory* — textbook-level treatment connecting RMT filtering to practical covariance-matrix cleaning for portfolio construction (denoising a covariance matrix before computing an optimal portfolio, not just for factor discovery).
- **Statistical arbitrage via PCA:** Avellaneda & Lee (2010), "Statistical Arbitrage in the U.S. Equities Market" — the canonical paper using PCA-derived eigenportfolios as the market-neutral basis for mean-reversion trading signals (an OU-process fit to the residual after removing PCA factors). This is a natural "next technique" for the trading-strategy side of your extended notebook; it is not the arXiv ID you had listed, so track it down directly (it circulated as an SSRN/NYU working paper before formal publication).

## 4. Characteristics-based and hybrid PCA/factor methods (your natural next step)

Your extended notebook already tries to combine a stock-characteristics matrix with PCA (Section 5-ii/5-v). The literature has a well-developed version of exactly this idea, more rigorous than post-hoc labeling of PCA loadings by inspecting which tickers load heavily:

- **Connor & Linton (2007)** and **Connor, Hagmann & Linton (2012)**, semiparametric characteristic-based factor models: instead of estimating loadings freely, model each stock's loading on factor *k* as a smooth (nonparametric) function of observed characteristics (size, value, momentum). This directly formalizes "PC1 looks like a size factor" into a testable statement instead of a narrative one.
- **Fan, Liao & Wang (2016), "Projected Principal Component Analysis in Factor Models"** (this is what your `1406.3836` link actually is) — extends the above by *projecting* the return panel onto the space spanned by observed characteristics before running PCA, which removes idiosyncratic noise before the eigendecomposition and gives much better finite-sample loading/factor estimates when your effective sample size is small (exactly your N/D≈1.1–0.2 problem — see `PROJECT_AUDIT.md` §2.3). Includes formal specification tests for "do the characteristics explain the loadings at all" (H₀¹) and "do they explain them *fully*" (H₀²) — precisely the kind of formal test that would upgrade your PC-labeling exercise from narrative to statistics.
- **Kelly, Pruitt & Su (2019), "Characteristics Are Covariances: A Unified Model of Risk and Return"** (Instrumented PCA / IPCA) — the modern industry/academic standard for this exact problem: loadings are linear (not just correlated) functions of observed characteristics, estimated jointly with the latent factors, with a built-in way to test whether characteristics have any residual "alpha" predictive power beyond their role as risk-loading instruments. This is the most natural "another technique" to add to the repository roadmap (see §6).
- **Kakushadze (2016), "101 Formulaic Alphas"** and **Harvey, Liu & Zhu (2016), "…and the Cross-Section of Expected Returns"** (the "factor zoo" / 151-strategies paper) — you're already using both correctly in the extended notebook's alpha-pooling section. Harvey-Liu-Zhu's multiple-testing framework (t-stat hurdles that rise with the number of factors tested) is worth applying explicitly to your own IC/Sharpe results before calling any individual alpha "significant."

## 5. Structural breaks, regime shifts, and correlation dynamics (directly relevant to §1.4 of the audit)

- **Chow (1960)** — you already cite and correctly implement the classical test; the issue is applying it to an autocorrelated, overlapping-window series without adjustment (see audit §1.4).
- **Newey & West (1987)** — heteroskedasticity- and autocorrelation-consistent (HAC) standard errors; the standard fix for exactly the overlapping-window problem your rolling PC1 series has. A HAC-corrected Chow-style test, or simply re-running the Chow test on non-overlapping 24-month blocks (giving you ~5–6 independent observations instead of 112 overlapping ones), would be a defensible middle ground.
- **Bai & Perron (1998, 2003), "Estimating and Testing Linear Models with Multiple Structural Changes"** — the standard way to let the data find a break date rather than fixing March 2020 a priori (your own limitations section already flags this as a caveat; Bai-Perron with the `strucchange` R package or `ruptures` in Python is the direct next step to try).
- **Andrews (1993) / Andrews-Ploberger** sup-F test for a break at an *unknown* date — a lighter-weight alternative to full Bai-Perron if you just want a confidence interval on the break date itself instead of assuming March 2020.
- **Longin & Solnik (2001), "Extreme Correlation of International Equity Markets,"** and **Ang & Chen (2002), "Asymmetric Correlations of Equity Portfolios"** — the empirical literature establishing that equity correlations rise in market downturns as a general phenomenon, not a COVID-specific one. Essential context for not overstating the COVID structural-break claim (audit §1.4, problem 3).
- **King & Wadhwani (1990), "Transmission of Volatility Between Stock Markets"** — early formalization of contagion/correlation spikes around crisis events, useful background for distinguishing a transient contagion spike from your claimed *permanent* regime shift.

## 6. Practical/implementation resources

- **Macrosynergy, "Using Principal Components to Construct Macro Trading Signals"** (2024) — a full worked example of PCA-based signal construction with proper walk-forward, expanding-window cross-validation and an explicit, honest discussion of PCA's practical drawbacks (uncorrelated ≠ independent; components can be economically uninterpretable; correlation structure is regime-dependent, so PCA loadings estimated in one period can become stale). Good model for how to *write up* the caveats section of a PCA study — notice how much space they give to what PCA does *not* solve.
- **QuantInsti / IBKR Campus PCA tutorials** — fine for sanity-checking your `sklearn`/`scipy` implementation choices (SVD vs. eigendecomposition, standardization conventions) against a second independent walkthrough.
- **MIT 18.S096 (Topics in Mathematics with Applications in Finance), Lecture 15 notes** — a compact, rigorous derivation of PCA and its use in finance at the level you want for the "mathematical foundations" section of the report; better citation than a blog post for the SVD/eigendecomposition equivalence you already state correctly.
